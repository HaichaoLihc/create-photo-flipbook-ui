# Rhythm across the book

A photobook unfolds in time. The spread is a moment; the turn is an edit. Design anticipation, interruption, accumulation, pause, and return so that a strong image gains meaning from its neighbors. A book can hold a sustained quiet or dense passage, but constant maximum intensity makes individual peaks indistinguishable.

## Edit before decorating

Start from the proposition selected in `creative-direction.md` through [creative ideation](creative-ideation.md). Sequence relationships rather than merely dividing photographs into topics: a gesture answered by a landform, a close detail following a distant scene, a repeated object changing scale, a quiet aftermath following motion. Leave some connections open for the reader.

State the proposition in a sentence that guides image treatment and selection. “A trip through these places” identifies subject matter; an idea such as a body becoming small against changing surfaces suggests scale, material, and transitions. Derive the proposition from this collection rather than from a generic travel or memory theme.

Select enough space for the story to breathe. Do not force every good photo into the edit, fill every leaf, or make every spread a self-contained promotional poster. An image may be powerful because it arrives late, appears small, or is followed by silence.

Save `sequence-plan.md` before generating. A compact table is enough. For each spread record:

- Role in the overall arc or movement and source images; what it receives from the preceding spread and sets up for the next.
- Page structure, left/right roles, separate image units, and the intended effect of the following turn. Distinguish a diptych, frame sequence, text/image arrangement, collage, and continuous full-spread scene instead of calling all of them “medium intensity.”
- Overall intensity and occupied area, including a genuinely quiet passage and a deliberate peak.
- Text presence, exact words, role within the book's connected text, and the applicable shared type role and placement; explicitly mark text-free spreads. Do not choose a new writing or type voice per spread.
- Image scale/count/treatment, page ground, and color coverage/saturation, including how the edit enacts the selected concept. Record the degree of artistic intervention separately from visual intensity: what remains photographic, what changes, and why. Plan sustained mixed passages as well as photographic rests and stronger transformations; do not leave artistic treatment only to the cover or one showpiece.
- Which motifs, patterns, objects, lines, and shapes appear, change, or stay absent.

Use descriptions or rough low/medium/high levels to expose repetition, not to compute an aesthetic score. The plan should reveal changes when read as a sequence. Avoid mechanically alternating loud/quiet or changing everything merely for variety.

Include a compact book-wide arc and a reading-order copy pass in the plan. Read title, observations, captions, and closing words together before generation; check thematic development, tone, references, and unnecessary repetition using [the writing guidance](default-style.md#write-one-connected-text). A wordless stretch is a deliberate part of that progression. Keep shared typography and writing rules in `creative-direction.md`, and reference their defined roles in each spread entry.

### When the requested book is longer

Honor the requested length without treating it as a quota of different photographs. Extend the central idea through movements with different durations: establish, gather, interrupt, revisit, release, or another structure the material suggests. A source can return as a different crop, scale, medium, or absence. Keep track of how that return changes its meaning. Additional locations and subjects should earn their place through relationships, not merely fill extra spreads. Judge the arc both within each movement and across the whole book; do not stretch a short loud/quiet cycle to fill thirty pages.

## Compose several rhythms at once

| Element | Useful changes across the sequence | Watch for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Text | silence → small note or caption → a brief emphasis → silence; changing amount, position, line length within the chosen voice | a large headline on every spread, uniformly tiny notes, compulsory numbering or slogans |
| Images | distant/close, one/many, small/immersive, cropped/isolated, photographic/abstract | identical image footprint, every subject blown up, every spread full bleed |
| Artistic intervention | intact photographic rest, selective transformation within a photographic passage, stronger abstraction or collage | untouched photo boxes throughout, every image redrawn, or the same effect stamped on every spread |
| Page structure | comparison across leaves, a sequence of frames, a text/image arrangement, an immersive passage | one continuous picture across every spread, changing colors while preserving the same structure |
| Color | sustained color world, shifts in ground or temperature, a collision, a smooth field, release | the whole palette on every page, automatic cream-paper resets, unrelated color changes for novelty |
| Motifs and objects | introduce, echo at another scale, withdraw, return transformed | logo-like repetition, the same cutout pasted throughout |
| Pattern and texture | dense repetition, sparse trace, smooth silence | coarse halftones or paper distress applied at equal strength everywhere |
| Lines and shapes | thin/thick, short/extended, directional/still, filled/open, absent/present | generic diagonal marks, disks, or borders repeated without purpose |
| Space | occupied/open, compressed/expansive, balanced/displaced | all empty space confined to the same corner or margin |

These rhythms need not peak together. A large image can be muted and text-free; a tiny bright object can command a mostly empty spread; an intimate note can follow a dense collage. Let one or two elements lead at a time. Avoid routinely combining giant text, saturated color, dense imagery, heavy patterns, and multiple motifs in the same spread.

## Page turns and visual memory

- Establish a few visual constants, then vary how strongly they are present. Keep the reader oriented without making the next spread predictable.
- Use asymmetry within a spread and across turns. A detail near the right edge can anticipate the next scene; a later echo can recall an earlier shape with changed meaning.
- Let impact passages earn their intensity through quieter neighbors. Breathing room can be open paper, a single small detail, a dark or colored field, muted imagery, a nearly empty leaf, or a simple image with no words or decorative marks. Keep rests inside the chosen visual world; quiet does not require a white or faded page.
- Vary the duration of pauses and accumulations. A quiet sequence may last more than one spread; a peak can arrive abruptly. Do not reduce pacing to an alternating template.
- Let the closing passage release or transform an earlier idea. It need not be another headline, visual climax, or miniature catalogue of everything used.

## Review the sequence, then revise

Before extending a normal-length book, generate the outside cover, an impact spread, a quiet spread, and a representative medium-intensity passage from the plan. Choose actual planned spreads, not extra deliverable pages. For a very short book, review the available roles together. Compare the pilots at two distances. As thumbnails, the sequence should show changes of page structure, mass, direction, edge, color, and silence. At reader size, inspect image specificity, edge relationships, spacing, material detail, and legible type.

Check the front cover on its own as well as the full outside-cover artwork. Its composition, ground, and image behavior should introduce the selected concept; a pale field with a small centered photo must be an intentional choice. Check that the cover and opening belong to the same book without requiring every interior spread to repeat the cover's composition.

The impact/quiet contrast must be perceptible, but that alone is insufficient. For the default zine, the middle pilot must combine recognizable photography with a visible, meaningful artistic operation and a considered left/right relationship. Photo boxes with a colored ground and caption do not test this balance; neither does a fully transformed seamless scene. The operation should connect source, image, and page rather than supply a token decoration. A quiet pilot can remain largely photographic, and the impact pilot may transform more extensively; the three should share a recognizable artistic vocabulary. If the pilots miss either side of the balance, revise the relevant operation and prompt, then regenerate the affected pilot before extending that approach. This is an agent-owned review, not a request for user approval.

During production, review each new spread with its neighbors; where one is not generated yet, compare against that neighbor's plan and revisit the transition when it exists. Assess the preceding/current/following passage as well as the whole-book arc. After revising a spread, recheck both adjacent transitions. If an accepted departure changes the intended rhythm or text thread, update the plan and affected prompts rather than allowing later spreads to follow a stale brief.

Review all accepted artworks in order on a contact sheet and through the reader. Also inspect individual left/right leaves: a quiet whole spread can conceal two equally crowded pages, and mobile viewing changes what is seen together.

Ask where the eye slows, where it accelerates, what returns, and what is deliberately withheld. At thumbnail size, do repeated giant text blocks or color fields flatten the arc? Is the quietest passage noticeably quieter in several dimensions? Are motifs evolving rather than accumulating? Do transitions follow the content rather than a decorative formula?

Inspect the quieter and medium passages for equal care: do pairings, crops, and artistic operations create discovery, or could their images be swapped without changing anything? Is the sequence dominated by untouched photo boxes or, at the other extreme, continuous invented scenes? Is its artistic character sustained beyond the cover? Is type active and readable where it appears, or always a tiny phrase floating over a scene? Are print effects concealing weak page composition? Does close viewing reveal intentional relationships while pauses remain unburdened? Review adjacent sequences while producing the book so an unsuitable visual language is corrected before it spreads.

Compare the actual pilot with its planned structure and treatment. If intended image relationships have melted into one scene, a meaningful transformation has disappeared into plain photo placement, or a text hierarchy has vanished, record a miss and revise. Intentional overlap or a source-derived mark crossing a margin is valid when it serves the composition. Do not rationalize a missing artistic operation solely because the result is tidy or helps the loud/quiet arc.

Audit typography across all text-bearing spreads, including cover and colophon: family or hand, letterforms, case, weight, spacing, and role-based relative size. Scene changes should not produce unplanned type changes. Read all rendered words in order separately from the image review; check wording against the plan and whether the text sustains one theme and a natural, consistent voice. Correct type drift and disconnected copy as well as spelling errors. A deliberate change to the shared system must be applied to every affected spread.

Save a brief `rhythm-review.md` describing the actual arc, the pilot decisions, any intended-versus-generated differences, and revisions made. Refer to specific artworks and page turns rather than copying the plan or declaring everything successful. Regenerate spreads that flatten the sequence or fall below the established art direction. Individual beauty does not excuse a monotonous book; variation must be visible in the delivered artwork, not only stated in the plan. If review is unfinished, report it as unfinished.
