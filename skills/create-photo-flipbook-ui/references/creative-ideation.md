# Explore the book before designing its pages

Read this after inspecting the photos and before writing the sequence plan. The output is a chosen creative direction, not a catalogue of effects. Work as an artist: notice something particular, propose what it could mean, and invent a visual language that develops that idea through a book.

## Find a generative idea

Start with a few concrete observations tied to source images: a recurring obstruction, an odd color relationship, repeated gestures, a changing horizon, a contrast between polished surfaces and unruly bodies. Look for tension, surprise, or a question that the photographs leave open. Subject labels such as “summer trip,” “nature,” or “memories” are starting points, not sufficient concepts.

Move from observation to an artistic proposition and then to an editorial operation. For example, repeated windows might suggest a book about partial access: an obstructed photograph faces a clear view, a cropped detail precedes the whole frame, and a later turn reveals what was withheld. The idea determines how the reader encounters the photographs; window-shaped decoration alone would not develop it. Make this connection for the actual collection rather than borrowing the example automatically.

For the default photo zine, each concept should connect an editorial idea to a small artistic vocabulary. Develop image relationships, transformations, page structures, and type together. A metaphor may guide the edit without being literally illustrated, but an idea expressed only by arranging unchanged photos may be too conservative. Describe a characteristic middle passage where recognizable photography and a selective artistic operation work together, then how that same vocabulary can become quieter or more radical elsewhere.

Draw on knowledge of editorial design, artist books, print processes, cinema, painting, textiles, stage design, visual poetry, and vernacular graphics. Borrow an operation—montage, layering, repetition, masking, interruption, scale reversal—when it advances the proposition. A list of artist names or aesthetic adjectives is not an art direction. External moodboard research is optional unless the user requests it; ideation can start from the photographs and existing knowledge.

## Develop distinct possibilities

For an open brief, sketch several alternatives, usually three. Keep each compact but concrete:

- **Premise and evidence:** what the book explores, and which visible details make that reading plausible.
- **Image experience and page architecture:** how photographs are encountered—compared, partially revealed, fragmented, repeated, isolated, or immersed in—and why. Describe a representative spread's left/right roles, image units, margins, crops, and reading order. State what remains photographic, what is transformed, and how their interaction gives the page its artistic character.
- **Visual language:** the page ground, color relationships, material behavior, typography, and any source-derived elements or motifs. A motif may be an interval or framing rule, not necessarily an added icon.
- **Cover and movement:** a specific front/back cover idea, an opening, a characteristic middle passage, and a turn that changes the reader's experience. Describe how this direction can sustain a sequence.

Separate the alternatives in their underlying idea, image behavior, and book structure. Three pale collages with different accent colors offer no real choice; three different surreal scenes may still share the same compositional formula. Within the user's constraints, explore at least one route that challenges the first obvious treatment. When the brief already specifies a direction, explore meaningful interpretations of that direction instead of inventing unrelated styles.

### Ways to stretch the imagination

These are starting mechanisms, not a menu to assign to every collection. Invent, combine, or discard them according to what the sources suggest.

| Source cue or tension | Possible book logic | Consequences for the artwork |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Windows, doorways, occlusion, glimpses | **A book of thresholds** | Crops sit inside shifting apertures; a cover with a narrow opening precedes a later immersive reveal. The framing changes what can be known. |
| Rock, rust, wear, layered surfaces | **An excavation** | Enlarged detail crops sit beside whole frames; stacked image strips suggest strata while retaining their boundaries. A later turn reveals the source of an earlier fragment. |
| Reflections, night light, isolated bright signs | **Signals in darkness** | Ink-dark grounds, luminous photographic islands, sparse sharp type, and interrupted image sequences. A rest can be one dim reflection on black. |
| Repeated gestures, crowds, motion, routes | **A visual score** | Image strips accelerate, repeated frames establish a beat, a held image interrupts it. Spacing and scale carry timing; the cover compresses a gesture into a graphic pulse. |
| Repeated objects with small differences | **A cabinet of variations** | A measured comparison gives way to one radically enlarged specimen. Source silhouettes become dividers or containers. Use factual labels only when supported. |
| Strong color shifts, light, atmosphere | **Chromatic weather** | Photographs retain their different light while colored page fields connect or oppose them. A source detail printed at another scale makes a color relationship newly visible. |
| Fabric, tiles, signs, repeated local forms | **A pattern that breaks** | Source details become a repeat that photographs interrupt; pattern density builds, then stops. The cover can be an all-over pattern whose origin is discovered inside. |
| Vast scenes and overlooked small details | **An argument about scale** | A detail occupies a whole spread while a panorama shrinks to a small interval; later turns reverse the relationship. Scale, rather than added ornament, carries the surprise. |

Other structures may be more appropriate: a parallel story across left and right leaves, an image revealed over successive turns, a book that moves from sharp description into abstraction, or a collection held together by one recurring shape. Do not combine every device into one book.

Keep physical ideas compatible with the deliverable. Apertures, translucent sheets, unusual folds, metallic ink, or stitched surfaces can be represented within flat artwork; do not promise actual die-cuts, transparency, alternate bindings, or new reader mechanics unless requested and implemented.

## Choose and commit

Choose the direction with the strongest relationship to the sources, the most promising page turns, and enough range to sustain both intensity and rest. Novelty alone is insufficient. White, black, saturated color, or intact photographs can all be right when they serve the chosen idea. Do not replace a pale house style with a compulsory maximalist one.

Save `creative-direction.md` alongside the book outputs. Include the observations, compact alternatives, the selection rationale, and the decisions production needs:

- The governing proposition, main editorial operation, and how it works without a written explanation.
- The cover's composition and its relationship to the opening and ending. Treat it as an expression of the idea; consider an immersive crop, typographic construction, pattern, emblem, or material field when appropriate.
- Page ground and color logic, including whether these remain constant or change by movement. Empty space may be dark, colored, photographic, or textured; decide its visual weight deliberately.
- A small artistic vocabulary: source-derived operations, material behavior, recurring elements, and how they evolve or disappear. Include spatial decisions such as alignments, image edges, and text/image relationships. Explain how the style remains visible beyond the cover and how its degree of intervention varies through the sequence.
- One book-wide typographic system: primary family or consistent hand, defined title/body/caption roles as needed, relative sizes, weights, case, spacing, and alignment logic. A second voice needs a necessary, named function; individual scenes do not each get their own type style.
- One writing brief: the theme or question the text develops, point of view, tense where relevant, register, language, and source of factual or personal details. Explain what words add to the images and how the opening, middle, and closing relate. A mostly wordless book can keep this brief short.
- A characteristic editorial spread, a surprising turn, and how a quiet passage works inside this same visual world. Vary page structures as well as intensity; avoid a sequence consisting only of full-spread scenes and isolated cutouts on empty fields.
- The tempting habits that would weaken this particular concept, such as soft dissolving edges, uniform frames, or a miniature photo centered on every cover.

A concise note is enough; do not generate several complete books to choose a concept. Share the chosen idea briefly and continue without an approval gate unless the user asked to select. Carry the decisions into [book rhythm](book-rhythm.md) and [spread generation](spread-generation.md).

## Test the idea in the artwork

Compare the cover and interior pilots with the creative direction. Could a reader recognize the premise from the image relationships and page turns without reading the explanation? Would swapping in an unrelated photo set leave the design essentially unchanged? Do ordinary pages visibly combine photographic character with an artistic language, or do they fall back to untouched boxes or continuous invented scenes? Does the quiet spread belong to this book, or has it reverted to a generic empty field? If the concept exists only in the note, revise the visual operation before expanding the sequence.

When earlier runs are available, compare their cover silhouettes, dominant grounds, image presentation, and recurring marks. If the new book looks interchangeable, reconsider the premise or its execution; changing only the title and accent color will not fix it. Judge against the user's brief and sources, not difference for its own sake.
