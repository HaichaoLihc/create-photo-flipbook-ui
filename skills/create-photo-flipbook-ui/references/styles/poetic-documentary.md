# Poetic Documentary

Use for fine-art collections built from attentive observation of a place, journey, community, or everyday life. Favor an emotional or psychological current over exhaustive coverage.

This style synthesizes high-level photobook principles associated with Alec Soth's *Sleeping by the Mississippi*, Robert Frank's *The Americans*, and Rinko Kawauchi's *Illuminance*. Do not imitate any artist's signature images or reproduce a specific book design.

## Edit

- Select rigorously. Keep only photographs that strengthen the book; do not include every usable image.
- Mix people, landscapes, interiors, objects, gestures, and quiet details when the collection supports them.
- Value overlooked, ambiguous, or transitional images alongside obvious heroes.
- Find a recurring emotional condition or motif rather than forcing a literal plot.

## Sequence

- Sequence associatively rather than by filename or strict chronology.
- Link photographs thematically, conceptually, formally, or emotionally.
- Let motifs recur with variation: roads, windows, hands, water, signs, empty rooms, faces, or light.
- Alternate social observation with private or atmospheric images.
- Build a loose journey with an intentional opener, developing echoes, one or two peaks, breathing room, and a resonant ending.
- Use page turns to reveal, interrupt, or reframe meaning. Avoid explaining every transition.

## Pair and pace

- Pair through gesture, color, line, scale, atmosphere, contradiction, or conceptual echo—not merely shared subject matter.
- Allow unequal pairings and single images facing quiet or blank pages.
- Vary wide, medium, and detail views; brightness; density; human presence; and emotional temperature.
- Use supporting images as connective tissue. Not every image or spread should announce itself.

## Compose

- Keep a restrained vocabulary: one image on a page, one image across a spread, two images in dialogue, and occasional blank or text-only pauses.
- Prefer consistent margins, quiet off-white paper, and photography-led spreads.
- Use full bleed sparingly and only when immersion or interruption matters.
- Keep important subjects clear of the gutter. Consider gaze and directional movement across the spread.
- Avoid decorative grids, arbitrary scale changes, scrapbook effects, and layout novelty without editorial purpose.

## Type and cover

- Keep typography minimal, elegant, and consistently modest. Prefer one understated serif or neutral sans serif family; use no more than two families.
- Keep interior titles, captions, folios, and text small or medium. Avoid oversized quotes, section headings, numerals, or magazine-like display type.
- Reserve large type primarily for the front cover and use it rarely elsewhere.
- Isolate any large type with generous whitespace. Do not place small kickers, captions, metadata, folios, or labels around it; suppress nearby secondary text rather than creating a large-versus-tiny hierarchy.
- Keep each page or spread typographically quiet, with as few sizes and weights as possible.
- Avoid captions beside every image. Put necessary locations, dates, or notes in restrained end matter when possible.
- Use a quiet cover: one clear title treatment and one source-derived surface color or one carefully chosen photograph. If the title is large, let it stand alone.
- Match the empty back-cover surface to the front.

## Critique

On the spread contact sheet, reject:

- repetition without development;
- too many adjacent landscapes, portraits, or full-bleed heroes;
- pairings that merely duplicate subject matter;
- a sequence that reads as filename order or a slideshow;
- decorative layouts competing with photographs;
- oversized interior typography or small text clustered around display type;
- an opener that explains too much or an ending that merely stops.
